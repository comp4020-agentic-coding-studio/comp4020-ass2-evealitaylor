# The Log

Every Shift from week 2 produces something, and it goes here. Ten entries by
week 11: the norm map, the first edit, the revert, the sourcing dossier, the
closing rationale, the queue shifts, the audit.

Forty-five percent of the Log's mark is on the annotation rather than on the
contributions, which surprises people. The reason is below.

## A week 4 entry

Published with the author's permission, lightly trimmed. Their project was
English Wikipedia; their home topic was regional Victorian railway stations.

### The contribution

Edit to *Maldon railway station, Victoria*, 8 March, 14:22.

```
Changed: "The station closed in 1976."
To:      "Regular passenger services ceased in 1976, though the line
          remained in use for freight until 1985."

Summary: closure date refers to passenger services only; freight
         continued to 1985 per VR annual report cited
```

### What I predicted

> This one is safe. I have a primary source, the existing sentence is
> straightforwardly wrong, and I have used the summary line properly this time.
> If anything happens it will be someone asking for a better citation. Nobody
> has edited this article since 2021.

### What actually happened

Reverted at 14:31. Nine minutes.

The summary on the revert was four words: `see talk, VR annual reports`.

### What I had misread

Three things, in increasing order of how much they embarrassed me.

The small one: the article was not unwatched. It had not been *edited* since
2021. Thirty-one people had it on a watchlist, most of them from a WikiProject
covering Victorian rail, and the person who reverted me was one of them.

The middle one: I had read the Victorian Railways annual report as a source
about the station. The editor who reverted me had read the same report and knew
something I did not, which is that VR's annual reports use "closed" to mean
withdrawn from the public timetable, and that the heritage railway operating the
line after 1985 makes "remained in use for freight" wrong in a way my source
could not tell me. My citation was real, my reading of it was not, and the
difference is exactly the one week 5 is about.

The big one, and the reason this entry is longer than the others: I had spent
week 2 building a norm map of this WikiProject and had written down, in my own
handwriting, that they revert first and discuss on the talk page. Then I got
reverted and my first reaction was that it was rude. I had recorded the norm
and still experienced it as a personal judgement, which suggests that knowing a
community's rules and having internalised them are further apart than week 2
made it sound.

### What I did next

Posted on the talk page at 15:40 — longer than it needed to be, and the first
draft, which Ren made me rewrite in the Shift, opened by pointing out that I had
cited a primary source. The posted version asked what the annual report's
"closed" actually covered.

Reply came the next morning, from the reverting editor, with a scan of a 1983
working timetable and the sentence: *"the freight thing is a common mistake, the
article's been wrong in the other direction before"*.

The agreed wording went in on 10 March and is still there.

## Why the annotation carries the marks

The contribution above is worth almost nothing on its own. One sentence, in one
article, about one station, and the version that survived was mostly written by
somebody else.

What it is evidence of is a student who predicted wrong, could say precisely
where the prediction failed, and noticed that their emotional reaction had run
ahead of knowledge they already had written down. That is the thing this course
is trying to produce, and it is only visible in the annotation.

A log full of accepted edits and no annotation reads as somebody who got lucky
or stayed safe. Both are worse positions to be in at week 11 than having been
reverted six times and understood it by the fourth.

## What a weak entry looks like

For calibration. Same week, same assignment, different student:

> **Week 4.** Made an edit to the article. It was reverted by another editor who
> disagreed with my change. I posted on the talk page to explain my reasoning.
> This taught me about the importance of consensus in online communities.

Everything in it is true. Nothing in it is specific, nothing in it could only
have been written by someone who was there, and the last sentence is the kind of
thing you can write without having made an edit at all.

That entry received 4 out of 10.

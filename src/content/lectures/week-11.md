Bots have been the highest-volume editors on Wikipedia since the mid-2000s and
nobody objects, because the bots do work no one wants: fixing link rot,
standardising date formats, reverting obvious vandalism within seconds. They
are approved through a process, they have operators with names, and they can be
switched off.

OSM's version of the argument is about imports. Bulk-loading a government road
dataset adds in an afternoon what surveyors would take years to map, at the
cost of data nobody on the ground has checked and a local community who were
not consulted. The project has been arguing about this, with unusual
politeness, since the TIGER road import landed across the United States in
2007.

The new problem is different in kind. Generated prose is cheap, plausible,
correctly formatted, and sometimes cites sources that do not exist. Assessment
is the scarce resource in every volunteer project, and generated content attacks
exactly that: it takes an editor twenty minutes to check what took four seconds
to produce.

Which raises a question you should sit with, given how this course is taught and
what you will use to draft your own submissions: the asymmetry is not between
humans and machines. It is between producing and checking, and it was already
there.

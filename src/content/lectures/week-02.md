A subreddit's sidebar has eight rules. Its moderators enforce about thirty. The
other twenty-two are real, consistently applied, and discoverable only by
watching what gets removed — which is to say, discoverable only by reading the
record of other people's mistakes.

This is not a failure of documentation. Some of the twenty-two are too
embarrassing to write down. Some are contested inside the mod team. Some were
never articulated at all, and exist as a shared instinct about what this place
is for, which is precisely the thing a written rule cannot hold.

Anthropologists have a word for the method that recovers them, and it is not a
word that makes the practice sound as passive as it looks from outside.
Lurking, done properly, is fieldwork: you read the modlog, you read the deleted
contributions, you read the talk-page archives, and you build a description of
the norms that the community itself could not have given you.

You do this before you post. Every contributor who ever complained that a
community was hostile to newcomers skipped this step, and some of them were
right anyway.

## Outline

- written rules, enforced rules, and the delta
- where the public record actually lives: modlogs, deletion logs, talk archives,
  closed pull requests
- reading a removal for the rule behind it
- legitimate peripheral participation, and why the reading is the joining
- the limits: what lurking cannot tell you, and when to just ask
